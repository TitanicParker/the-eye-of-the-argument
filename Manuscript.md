# What Happened to the Feet

* A reconstruction of the 2017 referral, investigation and explanation *

On 29 June 2017 a GP sent University Hospital Galway a patient with a broad and deteriorating problem. The referral did not describe a single neurological sign. It described an unusual festinant and stiff gait in a man attending with bilateral foot problems who attributed the gait to corns and callouses; the GP added that the feet were “not in a good state.” Over recent weeks, movement initiation had become difficult, stiffness and tone had increased in all four limbs, reflexes were brisk and there was possible wrist cogwheeling. The patient said he could not relax his muscles, especially in bed, where he clawed his toes and could not get comfortable. His dexterity was worsening. He was struggling with gowning and ungowning at work, sorting papers and repetitive manual tasks such as fitting scalpel blades to handles. The GP was concerned that the condition was deteriorating and affecting employment and daily life. fileciteturn60file0L5-L29 fileciteturn59file5L579-L604

The hospital record also contains a much shorter Reason for Referral. It reduced the operational statement to a 39-year-old with difficulty initiating movement, increased tone and stiffness in all four limbs and cogwheeling at the wrist. fileciteturn57file7L591-L607 There was nothing inherently improper about compression. A Reason for Referral is not required to reproduce the source letter. The significance lies in what happened next. The full referral represented the patient as a historical, functional and lower-limb problem involving feet, gait, toe behaviour, muscle tension, dexterity and decline. The shorter Reason for Referral represented him principally as a neurological motor syndrome. The subsequent investigation became extraordinarily deep in that second direction.

The specialist examination itself did not remain trapped inside the short précis. It recovered much of what the compression had omitted and added important new information. Tone was markedly and relatively symmetrically increased. Reflexes were moderately brisk. There was little tremor, though a small right lower-limb resting tremor eventually appeared. The gait was “strikingly abnormal.” The patient walked with an almost footdrop-like appearance that the specialist explicitly distinguished from true weakness: it was “somewhat suggestive of a dystonic posture — NOT FOOT DROP.” There was no arm swing, abnormal upper-limb posturing, lead-pipe rigidity, axial rigidity while walking and gross bradykinesia. fileciteturn60file1L35-L54 Structural abnormalities of the feet were also directly recorded: pes planus and hammertoes. fileciteturn60file2L61-L72

That recovery matters because the case is not that the broader problem vanished. It did not. Examination restored the abnormal gait and brisk reflexes, confirmed severe tone and rigidity, added bradykinesia and characterised the lower-limb movement much more precisely. It also established objective foot abnormalities. By the end of examination, therefore, the hospital knew that the same patient had a major neurological gait and movement disorder and structural abnormalities of the feet. The historical record still contained bilateral foot problems, corns and callouses, toe clawing, inability to relax and a long story of gait and functional deterioration. What had not been established with equivalent depth was the relationship between those facts.

The record did not demonstrate that the structural feet were independent of the neurological gait disorder. It did not demonstrate that neurological posture or abnormal loading had caused the foot pathology. It did not establish whether neurological posture aggravated genuine structural lesions. It did not establish whether the historical toe clawing was dystonic. It did not establish whether the painful and structural foot problem and the neurological lower-limb problem were two processes or a mixed process. The evidence established coexistence more strongly than separation. This is a documentary conclusion, not a diagnosis: the findings are in the record; the relationship between them was not yet resolved.

The investigation then became exceptionally sophisticated around severe, unusually young and atypical Parkinsonism. The CT request described a 39-year-old with “severe Parkinsonism but no obvious cause” and sought to exclude serious intracranial pathology. fileciteturn60file3L79-L101 The team obtained MRI brain and cervical spine imaging, a DaTscan, abdominal ultrasound, an extensive haematological panel, inpatient physiotherapy and occupational therapy input, and discussed the patient at a Neurology complex-case meeting. The plan expressly considered recessively inherited neurogenetics, while treatment strategy was shaped by concern that disease onset might have been in the patient’s mid-twenties. fileciteturn61file0L8-L27 This was not an unsophisticated admission. It was focused, serious and diagnostically ambitious.

The DaTscan request shows what problem the team believed it was investigating. It described a very young man with bilateral Parkinsonism, gross bradykinesia, little tremor, a presentation atypical in several respects, possible symptoms extending back thirteen years, and a walk that was “almost dystonically unusual.” The request then stated that an autosomal-recessive Parkinsonian disorder was suspected. fileciteturn59file9L871-L890 The treating account later made the genetic reasoning explicit. Very young onset, akinetic rigidity, lack of a dominant family history, marked symmetry and preserved cognition were cited as reasons to ask whether the patient had a PRKN gene disorder, PARK2; PARK6, PARK7 and PARK9 were also considered. fileciteturn58file3L56-L70

This matters because PRKN/PARK2 and dystonic lower-limb phenomenology were already inside the clinicians’ own reasoning in 2017. That is not hindsight. By then, the medical literature had long associated parkin mutations with early-onset disease, symmetry, dystonia and hyperreflexia; a major 2000 series reported that dystonia in mutation-positive patients usually began in the lower limbs and stressed that phenotype alone could not make the molecular diagnosis. citeturn161701search0 European recommendations published in 2013 also stated that parkin, PINK1 and DJ-1 testing should be considered in very-early-onset sporadic Parkinson’s disease, particularly with onset before 40. citeturn161701search4

The knowledge was also close to the treating environment. In 2015, Patrick Browne and Consultant Neurologist Timothy Counihan of Galway University Hospital co-authored an article specifically on the genetics of Parkinson’s disease, including PRKN. Counihan also co-authored Galway material on dystonia and had a substantial published movement-disorders record. citeturn458417search0turn458417search1turn458417search2 The surviving 2017 correspondence itself refers to “Tim” in consultation; the contemporaneous page does not identify his surname, and the canonical record notes that the identification as Professor Timothy Counihan comes from later clinician material. fileciteturn57file0L31-L43 That qualification matters. But if that later identification is accepted, the professional environment contained specific pre-existing knowledge of the phenotype then being discussed.

The external opinion obtained soon afterwards is even harder to dismiss as hindsight. Professor Daniel Healy had already published in familial and early-onset Parkinson genetics, including a clinician’s guide to familial parkinsonism and work on recessive Parkinson genes such as PINK1. citeturn161701search1turn161701search2turn161701search3 After personally seeing the patient in September 2017, Healy wrote: “I think probably has Park II” and recommended testing PARK2 first, followed by a broader panel if it was negative. fileciteturn57file0L45-L60 fileciteturn57file2L164-L194 That opinion does not prove that the July team molecularly knew the patient had PRKN disease, and it does not establish negligence. It establishes something narrower: PARK2 was a serious contemporaneous medical interpretation of this patient, not an explanation invented after later genetics.

Once that is understood, the full GP referral acquires a different significance. The referral did not diagnose PRKN and did not prove that the foot pathology was neurological. Corns and callouses are not PRKN signs. Pes planus and hammertoes were real structural findings. But the GP had supplied an earlier history of abnormal/festinant/stiff gait, bilateral foot problems, toe clawing, inability to relax and progressive dysfunction. The specialist then directly observed a dystonic-looking pseudo-footdrop in a very young patient whose team was already considering autosomal-recessive Parkinsonism and PRKN. On the medical knowledge available at the time, that combination created an obvious relationship question: did some part of the feet–toe–gait history belong to the same lower-limb movement disorder, did structural disease contribute independently, or were both mechanisms present?

The surviving investigation is much stronger on the existence and cause of the Parkinsonian syndrome than on that relationship. The team generated new evidence about Parkinsonism through examination, brain and cervical imaging, dopaminergic imaging, laboratory work, case discussion, genetics and treatment planning. The available record does not show a comparably developed direct effort to discriminate whether the dystonic-looking lower limb contributed to the foot posture, whether the toe clawing was dystonic, whether abnormal loading aggravated the corns and callouses, or whether a peripheral nerve disorder independently explained the pain. The point is not that peripheral neuropathy was medically impossible. It plainly was a legitimate possibility. The point is that it remained a possibility when the discharge account needed to make the broader patient coherent.

That is where the two representations of the patient meet. The admission had been organised around a narrower neurological motor problem, but the final specialist account had to report back on the whole presentation. The unresolved remainder still required a place. It could have been further investigated; it could have remained explicitly unresolved; it could have been allocated to another service; or it could be organised by clinical explanation. The record shows all of the latter three to some degree, but explanation did most of the conceptual work.

The completed account states that the leg symptoms, including corns, callouses and burning, “may have been completely separate” or were “perhaps a secondary peripheral neuropathy,” perhaps related in some respect to previous significant alcohol intake, immediately adding: “Not clear as this has improved.” It then says that the patient had “in his own mind — markedly divorced” the predominant feelings of leg tension from the corns, callouses and foot pains. fileciteturn58file1L8-L23 The possible neuropathy also appears in the genetic discussion as “PARK 9 (the neuropathy question).” fileciteturn58file3L56-L69 Podiatry supplied the structural foot problem with a practical destination.

These were expressly tentative propositions. The record did not say peripheral neuropathy had been proved. It would therefore be inaccurate to turn “perhaps” into a confirmed diagnosis or to allege fabrication without evidence. The stronger and better-supported allegation is that this provisional explanation performed work that relationship-specific investigation had not completed. It gave the unresolved foot territory a coherent place: the central Parkinsonian syndrome could continue down the neurological and genetic pathway, while pain, burning, corns, callouses and other foot problems could be treated as separate or peripheral, possibly alcohol-related, and suitable for Podiatry. The final explanation was broader than the direct investigation beneath it.

The difference is important precisely because the doctors had shown how much diagnostic capacity they could deploy. They distinguished a dystonic-looking pseudo-footdrop from true foot drop. They recognised severe young-onset Parkinsonism. They investigated structural, metabolic and genetic causes. They expressly raised PRKN. Yet the surviving record shows less discrimination when the problem became the relationship between that dystonic-looking lower limb and the painful, structurally abnormal feet around it. The allegation is not that all of the feet were neurological. Structural disease was genuine. The unresolved possibility was interaction: abnormal tone, posture and gait could potentially coexist with, contribute to, or aggravate real mechanical pathology. That mixed possibility is exactly what a simple neurological-versus-structural binary would miss.

The explanation then survived. Structural foot pathology continued to be documented, but structural pathology is not itself peripheral neuropathy. Pregabalin was used extensively, but the fact that a neuropathic-pain drug was prescribed cannot establish the mechanism of the pain. In March 2023, while taking 600 mg of pregabalin a day, the patient was still reported as having debilitating bilateral foot and leg pain, worsening over months, with “no quality of life.” fileciteturn55file1L44-L57 The urgent GP referral that month described severe bilateral burning in the feet and lower legs, at times extreme and “causing death wish”; even at 200 mg three times daily, pregabalin was said only just to be controlling the symptoms. fileciteturn55file6L234-L251 This establishes the severity of the later suffering. It does not, by itself, determine its mechanism.

At the same time, neurological evidence repeatedly crossed the old boundary. During a planned withdrawal of procyclidine in 2017, the patient wrote contemporaneously that tension was building, his feet were restless and his walk was falling apart. fileciteturn57file5L436-L457 Later neurology records considered probable OFF dystonia and recognised that some abrupt symptom patterns were not characteristic of peripheral neuropathy. In 2025 the same clinic documented dyskinetic movement of the feet, severe morning foot pain and relief in the feet once Parkinson medication took effect. The patient described hobbling from bed, being unable comfortably to tie his runners, the first 150 steps as “torture” and mornings as “murder in the feet.” fileciteturn55file0L18-L29 Controlled-release Sinemet then produced what the treating neurologist described as “profound relief,” while acknowledging that maximum-dose pregabalin had had incomplete effect. fileciteturn62file2L205-L217

None of those later facts proves that every historical foot symptom was dystonia. A response to dopaminergic medication is not a universal mechanism test. Corns, callouses and deformity did not cease to exist. What the later record shows is that important territory originally left outside, or ambiguously beside, the movement disorder contained a genuine neurological component. The inherited peripheral explanation remained available even as the neurological side acquired increasingly concrete evidence. Put simply, the explanation survived more strongly in the record than it strengthened medically.

The human consequence belongs in the reconstruction because it is also part of the record. By 2023 the patient was describing debilitating bilateral foot and leg pain and no quality of life. The GP recorded pain extreme enough to cause a death wish. By 2025 the act of getting out of bed and beginning to walk was described in terms of torture and “murder in the feet.” fileciteturn55file1L44-L57 fileciteturn55file6L246-L251 fileciteturn55file0L27-L29 The record establishes that suffering. It does not establish that a different decision in 2017 would probably have prevented a particular physical or psychological injury; that is a separate causation question requiring expert evidence. But the suffering occurred during the years in which the foot problem remained incompletely resolved and continued to be organised partly through the inherited peripheral explanation.

Only at the end does the later genetic result belong. In 2026 diagnostic testing identified two pathogenic PRKN variants. A St Vincent’s neurology letter then stated that the patient’s Parkinsonism and dystonia were due to compound heterozygous mutations in the Parkin gene. fileciteturn62file0L56-L70 The laboratory report independently records the two pathogenic PRKN variants. fileciteturn62file3L233-L256 This does not permit the conclusion that the July 2017 team already knew the molecular diagnosis. It does not prove that every pain sensation, corn or callus was dystonic. It does show that the neurological possibility already visible in 2017 had biological substance: PRKN disease and dystonia were not ideas foreign to the founding admission.

The problem alleged here is therefore not that the hospital failed to recognise Parkinsonism. It recognised Parkinsonism with considerable sophistication. The problem is that the investigation became deep around the narrower neurological problem while the broader feet–toe–gait relationship remained unresolved. When the final account had to expand back out to the whole presentation the GP had referred, explanation was used to carry what investigation had not established. The dystonic-looking lower limb remained inside the genetic Parkinsonism inquiry, while the painful and structurally abnormal foot territory around it was provisionally placed outside it as separate or neuropathic before their relationship had been equivalently tested.

That proposition does not establish motive and does not amount by itself to a legal finding of negligence. It does not erase the genuine structural disease of the feet or exclude a mixed mechanism. It identifies the documentary and medical gap that remains once the records are placed in order. A broad clinical problem came in. A narrower operational problem was written. That narrower problem was investigated with considerable sophistication. Examination recovered enough of the broader picture to show that abnormal feet and a dystonic-looking neurological lower limb coexisted. Their relationship remained unresolved. The completed account then made the broader case coherent through separation, possible neuropathy, possible alcohol contribution, the patient’s own distinction and Podiatry. That provisional construction travelled for years even as neurological evidence increasingly crossed the boundary.

The GP referred a whole problem. The hospital investigated a narrower one. By discharge, the difference between those two problems still had to be accounted for. That difference was carried by explanation.

What was later proved genetically was not a neurological idea absent from the 2017 record. PRKN and dystonia were already there. The unresolved problem was the feet.
