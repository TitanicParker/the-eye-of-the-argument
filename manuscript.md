# What Happened to the Feet

*How a broad neurological referral became a narrower investigation, and how an unresolved foot problem acquired an explanation that outlived its evidential basis*

On 29 June 2017 a GP sent University Hospital Galway a referral that was broader than the problem later captured in the hospital's formal Reason for Referral. The GP described an unusual festinant and stiff gait in a man with bilateral foot problems, corns and callouses and feet said to be in poor condition. Over recent weeks, the GP wrote, it had become increasingly evident that he was having difficulty initiating movement, with marked stiffness and increased tone in the upper and lower limbs and brisk reflexes. There was possible wrist cogwheeling but no overt resting tremor. The patient said he could not relax his muscles, especially in bed, where he clawed his toes and could not get comfortable. His dexterity was worsening. At work he was struggling to gown and ungown for the clean room, sort papers and perform simple repetitive manual tasks such as fitting scalpel blades to handles. The GP's concern was not an isolated gait complaint or an isolated foot complaint. It was a deteriorating feet–gait–tone–movement–toe–dexterity–function problem.

The hospital's Referral/Reason field was much narrower. It reduced the presentation to a 39-year-old with difficulty initiating movement, increased tone and stiffness in all four limbs and cogwheeling at the wrist. Compression in a referral field is not itself the allegation. A précis cannot reproduce an entire letter, and no clinical record is required to duplicate every fact every time. The importance of the compression lies in what followed. The principal evidence-generating programme became a sophisticated investigation of severe, unusually young and atypical Parkinsonism. The patient underwent detailed neurological examination, CT, MRI, DaTscan, extensive laboratory testing, complex-case discussion, genetic differential reasoning and detailed treatment planning. The clinicians did not fail because they lacked neurological sophistication. The record demonstrates the opposite.

The examination itself was searching and clinically productive. Tone was markedly and relatively symmetrically increased. Reflexes were brisk. There was very little tremor, although a small right lower-limb resting tremor eventually appeared. The gait was described as "strikingly abnormal." There was no arm swing, bilateral lead-pipe rigidity, axial rigidity while walking, gross bradykinesia and abnormal posturing. Most significantly for what happened later, the specialist described the patient as walking with an almost foot-drop appearance that was "somewhat suggestive of a dystonic posture — NOT FOOT DROP." This was not a retrospective interpretation imposed years later. It was written into the 2017 examination. Nor did the dystonic quality immediately disappear from the neurological thinking. The request for the July DaTscan described the walk as unusually, almost dystonically abnormal while considering an autosomal-recessive Parkinsonian disorder.

The examination also recovered parts of the original GP problem that the Reason for Referral had lost. The abnormal gait reappeared with much greater specificity. Brisk reflexes reappeared. Structural abnormalities of the feet were directly observed as pes planus and hammertoes. Severe rigidity and bradykinesia were established rather than merely reported. What the examination did not do was determine how those findings related to one another. By the end of the examination the hospital itself had established two facts in the same patient: a major neurological lower-limb and gait abnormality, including dystonic-looking posture, and objective structural abnormalities of the feet. The historical record also contained bilateral foot problems, corns and callouses, toe clawing, inability to relax, and a longstanding story in which feet and gait had already become entangled. Coexistence was established. Independence was not.

That distinction is the centre of the case. The record did not demonstrate that the structural foot pathology was neurologically independent. It did not demonstrate that neurological posturing had caused the structural pathology. It did not establish whether abnormal posture and loading aggravated genuine corns, callouses or deformity. It did not establish whether the historical toe clawing represented dystonia. It did not establish whether there were two independent processes or a mixed mechanism. Structural foot disease was real and must remain real in any fair account. But the presence of real structural disease did not answer what relationship, if any, it had to an equally real neurological gait disorder affecting the same lower-limb territory.

The importance of that unresolved relationship increased because the neurological differential became unusually sophisticated. The patient was 39, with a history suggesting that symptoms may have begun years earlier. The syndrome was severely akinetic-rigid, relatively symmetrical, associated with brisk reflexes and little prominent tremor. Cognition was preserved. The clinicians themselves considered autosomal-recessive Parkinsonism and expressly raised PRKN/PARK2. Their written reasoning identified very young onset, akinetic rigidity, the absence of a dominant family history, marked symmetry and excellent cognition as reasons to ask whether this was a PRKN gene disorder. Other recessive PARK disorders were also considered.

This was not an obscure diagnosis discovered only after modern genetic testing. By 2017 lower-limb dystonia, early or very early onset, relative symmetry, hyperreflexia and akinetic-rigid presentations were already recognised features of PRKN/PARK2 disease. Published work had described lower-limb dystonia as a common feature and had reported dystonic gait phenomena that could resemble foot drop without true weakness. The knowledge was not merely somewhere in the international literature. If the later identification of "Tim" in the 2017 correspondence as Professor Timothy Counihan is accepted, a neurologist within the Galway professional environment had co-authored a 2015 publication specifically discussing PRKN associations including young onset, dystonia, hyperreflexia, symmetry and akinetic-rigid disease. The treating neurologist, Thomas Monaghan, later wrote that he was not himself a movement-disorders neurologist, but the reasoning recorded in the case shows that he and his colleagues understood the possibility of recessive genetic Parkinsonism and knew enough to seek outside advice.

That outside advice is an important historical control against hindsight. Within roughly two months of the admission, Professor Daniel Healy at Beaumont Hospital personally reviewed the patient. Healy had established expertise in familial and early-onset Parkinsonism and neurogenetics. On 12 September 2017 he wrote that he thought the patient "probably has Park II" and suggested testing that first, followed by a broader panel if negative. The point is not that Healy's opinion proves what the July team was obliged to conclude. It does not. The point is that PARK2 was a serious contemporaneous medical interpretation. It was not reconstructed from the eventual genetic result years later.

The full GP referral therefore mattered in a way the short précis did not capture. Once the specialist had directly seen a dystonic-looking lower limb in a very young patient whose clinicians were already considering PRKN, the earlier description of festinant and stiff gait, toe clawing, inability to relax, bilateral abnormal feet and progressive dysfunction acquired additional neurological significance. None of those historical features diagnosed PRKN. Corns and callouses were not PRKN signs. Toe clawing was not automatically dystonia. But the combination created a clear clinical question: did some of the longstanding foot, toe and gait history belong to the same lower-limb movement disorder that was now being seen directly, or were the structural and neurological problems separate, or mixed? The available record shows the question more clearly than it shows an answer.

The completed specialist account nevertheless had to explain the whole patient, not only the part that had received the deepest investigation. When it turned back toward the leg and foot symptoms, the account proposed that the corns, callouses and burning "may have been completely separate" or were "perhaps a secondary peripheral neuropathy," perhaps related in some respect to previous alcohol intake. The same passage added, "Not clear." It also recorded that the patient had, "in his own mind — markedly divorced" the predominant leg tension from the corns, callouses and foot pains. Podiatry provided a practical destination for the structural foot problem. The possible neuropathy even entered the genetic discussion as "PARK 9 (the neuropathy question)."

These words matter because they were tentative. It would be inaccurate to describe the 2017 account as having proved peripheral neuropathy. It did not. Nor does the record justify alleging that neuropathy was fabricated. It was a clinical hypothesis built from history, symptoms, interpretation and possible alcohol exposure. The difficulty is different. The paragraph created a coherent explanatory route for precisely the territory whose relationship to the dystonic-looking lower limb had not been investigated at anything like the depth applied to the Parkinsonian syndrome. The final document supplied explanatory sophistication where relationship-specific medical discrimination was comparatively thin.

That is the allegation in its most defensible form. The team had demonstrated enormous diagnostic capacity when investigating Parkinsonism. It distinguished true foot drop from a dystonic-looking imitation. It investigated structural brain and cord disease. It ordered functional dopaminergic imaging. It considered metabolic, autoimmune, infectious and genetic causes. It discussed the case collectively. It identified PRKN/PARK2. Yet when the unresolved question was the relationship between the abnormal neurological lower limb and the painful, structurally abnormal feet surrounding it, the surviving record contains substantially more interpretation than direct discrimination. Peripheral neuropathy was medically imaginable. The issue is why that possibility acquired enough authority to organise the foot territory before the competing neurological relationship had been equivalently worked through.

The explanation then travelled. Over the following years, structural foot pathology continued to be demonstrated, but structural pathology is not the same thing as peripheral neuropathy. Pregabalin was used extensively, but treatment with a neuropathic-pain drug did not establish mechanism. At an important point in March 2023 the patient was taking 600 mg of pregabalin a day and was still reported as having debilitating bilateral foot and leg pain, worsening over months, with "no quality of life." The urgent GP referral that month described severe bilateral burning in the feet and lower legs, at times extreme and "causing death wish." Those are facts about suffering and treatment burden. They do not by themselves determine whether the pain was peripheral, dystonic, structural, central or mixed.

Meanwhile neurological evidence repeatedly crossed the boundary that the 2017 account had provisionally drawn. During a planned withdrawal of procyclidine in 2017, the patient's contemporaneous account described tension building, his feet becoming restless and his walk falling apart. Later records explicitly connected clawing feet with increased tone and Parkinsonian gait. In 2023 the treating neurologist himself considered Parkinsonian dystonia, probably OFF dystonia, and stated that an abrupt overnight symptom pattern was not characteristic of peripheral neuropathy. Toe curling later persisted even when pain improved. Parkinson medication repeatedly changed the experience of the feet. By early 2025 the patient described morning foot pain so severe that the feet were too sore to tie his shoes; the first roughly 150 steps were "torture" and mornings felt like "murder in the feet." The record also says that once Parkinson medication reached his system he experienced relief in the feet.

Controlled-release Sinemet then produced what the treating neurologist himself described as profound relief. That response did not prove that every historic corn, callus, pressure lesion or burning sensation had been dystonia. Levodopa response is not a universal mechanism test, and mixed pathology remained possible. But the neurological side of the disputed lower-limb territory was becoming harder to treat as merely adjacent to the foot problem. The explanation of possible peripheral neuropathy remained conditional while evidence of medication-sensitive neurological involvement accumulated.

The psychological consequence of the unresolved problem became severe. The 2023 record contains the phrases "debilitating pain," "no quality of life" and an urgent GP report of pain causing a death wish. Later the patient described walking as psychologically consuming and as a "psychological mountain." His world narrowed around the act of walking and the anticipation of pain. By 2025 he described hobbling out of bed, being unable comfortably to tie footwear, the first 150 footsteps as torture and mornings as murder in the feet. These phrases must not be converted into a claim that one unproven clinical decision caused every later physical or psychological injury. Causation of that kind requires separate expert analysis. But the suffering itself is documented, and it occurred while the original foot problem continued to be managed within an explanatory structure whose peripheral component had never acquired proportionate direct confirmation.

The later genetics must therefore be used carefully. In 2026 testing identified two pathogenic variants in PRKN, and a St Vincent's neurology letter stated that the patient's Parkinsonism and dystonia were due to compound heterozygous Parkin mutations. This does not mean the July 2017 team molecularly knew the answer. They did not. It does not prove that every foot symptom was dystonic, or that structural pathology was irrelevant, or that the correct clinical response in 2017 can now be declared from hindsight. What it does establish is narrower and more important: the neurological possibility already visible in 2017 had biological substance. PRKN/PARK2 and dystonia were not ideas foreign to the founding admission. They were already present in the clinicians' own reasoning.

The clinical error alleged here is therefore not failure to recognise Parkinsonism. Parkinsonism was recognised with considerable sophistication. The allegation is that the hospital investigated the neurological problem it had compressed from the referral, but when it later had to account for the broader problem the GP had actually sent, interpretation was used to complete work that medical investigation had left unfinished. The dystonic-looking lower limb remained inside the genetic Parkinsonism inquiry. The painful and structurally abnormal foot territory around it was provisionally separated and organised through possible peripheral neuropathy, possible alcohol contribution, the patient's own distinction between leg tension and foot pain, and Podiatry, before the relationship between those territories had been equivalently discriminated.

That provisional construction then survived more strongly in the record than it strengthened medically. It remained capable of organising treatment and interpretation for years even as direct evidence establishing peripheral neuropathy did not grow proportionately and neurological evidence repeatedly crossed the original boundary. None of this proves motive. It does not establish negligence. It does not show that a mixed structural and neurological explanation was impossible. Those are separate questions. What the documents do support is a narrower allegation about information, investigation and explanation: a relationship was left unresolved, then given a provisional explanatory structure that acquired durable authority.

What was eventually proved genetically was not a neurological idea foreign to the 2017 admission. PRKN/PARK2 and dystonia were already there. The unresolved question was the feet.
